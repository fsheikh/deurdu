### Translator Note: 

*Today Maulana Abulkalam Azad is primarily remembered as a muslim politician who stood against the partition of the Indian subcontinent into what became modern republics of India and Pakistan (later also Bangladesh). He is a favorite of nationalists on both sides since one could selectively use his political arguments to justify one's own position. A little less remembered is the fact that in a predominantly passive muslim participation within the collective intellectual development of colonized Indian society, Azad was a towering exception. The essay below is authored by Malik Ram, himself a renowned Indian scholar of Urdu, and considered a specialist on Azad writings. It has been taken from a book compiling excerpts of various newspapers founded by Maulana Azad in the period from 1914-1927.*

---


It may be surprising for some that Maulana Abulkalam Azad's mother tongue was not Urdu but actually Arabic. His mother was an ethnic Arab; niece of Mufti Mohammed bin Tahir from Madina. It is almost certain that she spoke in Arabic to Azad. In his childhood there would have been little opportunity to learn any other language since the family was settled in Hejaz where not a whole lot of options to study Urdu were available. A little while later when formal education was started, the focus was primarily Arabic and Persian. Since the family was quite religious and considered pious by many muslims, the primary emphasis of early education was religious studies. He completed the [Dars-i-Nizami](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dars-i_Nizami) course at an early age of just fifteen years.

Even more surprising is that Maulana Azad entered the world of writing and composing long before he turned fifteen. He wrote his first bits of poetry in 1899 when he was barely eleven years old. Normally at this age, children find it difficult to even phrase their expressions in proper sentences so poetry while being so young points to an exceptional case. When Azad started poetry, according to prevalent tradition of the time he had to find a teacher. During those days, [Ameer Minai](https://www.rekhta.org/poets/ameer-minai/profile) and [Dagh Dehlvi](https://www.rekhta.org/poets/dagh-dehlvi/profile) were the most renowned poetry teachers. Each of them had hundreds of direct students. Initially Azad was associated with Dagh Dehlvi, this could be due the fact that Molana Azad's family also belonged to Delhi. Although he was born in Mekkah, his father Molana Khair-ud-Din's ancestral town was Delhi. He had his own house in the famous Kocha Pandit. In any case Maulana Azad started in the apprenticeship of Dagh Dehlvi, but this association could not continue after a few Ghazals. He then turned to Amir Minai but the same situation happened there. After a couple of Ghazals, Azad lost interest in the Apprenticeship. It seems like the issue was not linguistic or stylistic rather more about ideology and purpose of poetry. Both Dagh and Amir, undoubted masters of their craft, were focused on language in their poetry. There was little content depth to their work. Compared to Dagh, Amir was much more talented and could even rely on higher traditional prestige, but since Dagh was much more popular and famous, Ameer too started to copy his style. Anyway this is a separate topic suitable for another discussion.

After Ameer Minai, Azad sought correction in his poetry from Maulana Mohammed [Zaheer Ahsan Shauq](https://www.rekhta.org/authors/mohammad-zaheer-ahsan-shauq). This association lasted longer but this also didn't count since soon after Azad completely abandoned poetry in favor of full time prose writing. His entire poetry collection, whatever could be saved from scourge of time, consists of hardly 250-300 couplets.

I was recounting that Maulana Azad started poetry at the age of eleven. Exceptional, but not unheard of. We have other examples in literature where poets started really early. However the astonishing part in Azad's story is that he also got the idea to start a digest where he would invite Ghazals from all over the country on his poetic pattern (Tarah). He probably thought, parallel to fulfilling a hobby, the competition will help him practice and improve this craft. This line of thought from an eleven year old is quite extraordinary. Probably he published the first monthly digest "Neerang-e-Alam" in November 1899 from Kolkata. I haven't seen it personally but I knew the existence of at least one issue. This digest only contained poetry with no prose. "Neerang-e-Alam" didn't even last one full year. Afterwards he started another monthly called "Al-Misbah" again from Kolkata. This also didn't survive for long. Other than these two digests I am not aware of any other early publication.

Both of these aforementioned digests fall under the category of practice. You might have seen samples from renowned Calligraphers, wherein before actual writing, they spend time just drawing circles and writing alphabets in different styles. This is done to ensure that hand fumbles do not ruin the final product. These two digests also served as a practice scratchpad for Molana Azad's journalistic career.

Afterwards, in 1902 he started a new monthly magazine named "[Lisan-us-Sidiq](https://rekhta.org/ebooks/lisan-us-sidiq-ebooks)", again from Kolkata. This was very different from the two earlier publications. "Neerang-e-Alam" was purely a poetry digest without even having a prose section. As mentioned before, although I haven't been able to get my hands around any issue from "Al-Misbah" however the name suggests it was most likely religious in nature. Now when "Lisan-us-Sidiq" came out it was completely devoid of poetry. I have gone through its whole archive.

The first issue of "Lisan-us-Sidiq" was published on 20th November 1903. This issue outlined the following objectives:

- Initiate discussions around social reforms for Indian Muslims.
- Enhance Urdu's literary reach.
- Spread literary and intellectual awareness particular in Bengal region.
- Justified literary criticism on Urdu publications

At the time this monthly was launched, its honorable editor was merely fifteen years old. The objectives listed above didn't just remain lofty ideals. Instead he made the magazine true to its name. Not only was the quality of essays within this magazine of a higher calibre, they were also written with an enchanting linguistic style. Consequently, in no time the magazine made its place among the frontline publications of that time. Various old and reputable journals published glowing reviews about the new magazine. With the seriousness of topics and condensed diction being used, readers got the impression that the editor might be a seasoned journalist. The magazine ended up garnering attention from all over the country. [Anjuman-e-Himayat-e-Islam](https://ahislam.org/about-us/) was a reputed organization of that time in Lahore. It even exists today. Its annual functions used to be ostentatious ceremonies. The organizers had a practice of inviting scholars from outside their institute for keynote speeches. The quality of essays and oratory style in "Lisan-us-Sidiq" impressed the organizers to such an extent that they invited its editor to travel to Lahore and deliver a talk at the 1904 annual function. They could be forgiven to expect an experienced, middle-aged Islamic scholar as a guest. One could only imagine the shock they must have felt after receiving a beardless 15-16 years old Maulana Azad. The next day upon arrival, he delivered a talk on the topic of "Preaching Methodology in Islam". This session was held on April 01, 1904.

This was the event at which Azad met [Altaf Hussain Hali](https://www.rekhta.org/poets/altaf-hussain-hali/profile) for the first time. Their encounter is an interesting story in itself. Maulana Azad had arrived in Lahore a day before the official start of the conference; where he met [Syed Wahiddudeen Salim](https://www.rekhta.org/authors/syed-wahidudeen-salim) Panipati. When Salim realized this young man is the editor of "Lisan-us-Sidiq" he rightly considered it as a world marvel. He took Azad to Hali who was also there to participate in the conference. Before introducing Azad to Hali, Saleem asked Hali, "How old do you think this gentleman here with me would be?" Hali's careful and circumspect nature is famous, he reluctantly answered, "fairly young". On Salim's insistence to guess the age, he finally gave an estimate around fifteen/sixteen years. Only then Salim disclosed the fact that this young man is actually editor of "Lisan-us-Sidiq". Hali had also seen this journal and was an admirer of some of its articles. Like others he also thought the person at helm would be an experienced journalist. He was astonished to find out that such a young person is the editor of this monthly. The relationship founded between these two scholars on that day only grew with time with increasing mutual admiration and respect.

Unfortunately "Lisan-us-Sidiq" also died after eighteen months. Maulana Azad's restless nature couldn't allow him to stick with something for a very long time. It was not helped by the fact that he was constantly on the move during those years. Sometimes only one issue appeared for two months. We can see this pattern by observing that after the November 1903 issue, a December 1903 number came out. At which point, completion of the first volume was declared. During 1904 only nine issues were published; there was a joint issue for the months of August and September. This concluded the second volume. No other publication came out during 1904. In 1905 only one issue was published which was the joint number for April/May. After that chapter of "Lisan-us-Sidiq" was closed forever.

In 1905 Maulana [Shibli Nomani](https://www.rekhta.org/authors/shibli-nomani/profile) invited Azad to Lukhnow to help with the compilation and arrangement of a monthly magazine, "[Al-Nadwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Nadwa)",  representing the educational institute for muslim scholars, "Darul Uloom Nadwatul Ulema". This was purely a research and scholarly magazine. Being a flagship publication of Nadwatul Ulema, Molana Shibli himself was the editor-in-chief of the magazine and responsible for its content. Shibli as an author and critic was unmatched in the Urdu world at that time; considering this inclusion of Azad in the editorial board of "Al-Nadwa" is not only surprising, it is hard to find another similar example. Beyond Azad's literary prowess, it is also a reflection on his defining personality traits of courtesy and thoughtfulness.  Azad was associated with "Al-Nadwa" for six months between 1905 and 1906. Afterwards he broke this association due to a personal reason.

Meanwhile Azad became famous throughout India. He won a lot of admirers due to his work on "Lisan-us-Sidiq". One of them was a gentleman named Shaikh Ghulam Mohammed from Amritsar. He was the owner of a three-day paper, "Wakeel" from Amritsar. When he came to know about Azad's separation from the editorial board of "Al-Nadwa" he invited him to Amritsar offering him the editorship of "Wakeel".  Upon receiving this invitation, Azad traveled to Amritsar and started working with "Wakeel". He made some refreshing changes to the newspaper which resulted in its increased popularity and circulation. However due to a personal tragedy he had to return from Amritsar after a short while.

Azad had an elder brother, Maulana Abu Nasar Ghulam Yasin Aah. Both brothers were educated and raised on a similar standard. Their father was a known Sufi Peer and had a large spiritual following in the surroundings of Kolkata and Mumbai. He was preparing his elder son Ghulam Yasin Aah as his spiritual heir. Aah was also quite similar to his father in terms of his world view and demeanor. But who can interfere in God's Will? Aah travelled to Iraq and got sick. He came back to Mumbai to get better treatment but his condition got worse. His father took him back to Kolkata where he breathed his last. This was in the middle of 1906. When Azad was busy with "Wakeel" in Amritsar. Maulan Khairuddeen wrote to him that Azad should come back home and help him in running the household. While Azad was still contemplating this command, a man was sent to Amritsar to bring Azad back. Left with no choice, Azad travelled back to Kolkata. The Amritsar period lasted just eight months from April 1906 to November 1906. Although he had gone back to Kolkata to respect the wishes of his father, he was totally dissatisfied with the work assigned to him. Training of spiritual followers and giving religious advice was not his view of meaningful work. Journalism, on the other hand, attracted him and Shaikh Ghulam Mohammaed was also very satisfied with his work. After some days, he openly told his father he could not keep up with this spirituality business. Not only did he dislike the work, he could not stand people kissing his hand with divine devotion. His father was a wise man. He realized the untenability of the situation. Instead of forcing Azad he gave him the permission to go back to Amritsar. Subsequently Azad returned to Amritsar in August 1906 where editorship of "Wakeel" was handed over to him again, however now his health started suffering. He could hardly stay in Amritsar for a year and in July 1908 separated from the magazine permanently.

At this point he was around 20 years old. He had worked in many magazines and newspapers. Some under his self-ownership and others where he was paid to do the work for others. Regardless of the working arrangements, he always had a higher ambition. He struggled and strived so that Journalism could be utilized for the betterment, service and wellbeing of the country and its people. These magazines and newspapers were like a laboratory for him where he tried to make sense of what should be the main purpose of a newspaper. Finally he found out that the objective he was looking for was not to be found in the outside world! His ambition and purpose found a destination in the self-launched weekly "[Al-Hilal](https://www.rekhta.org/emagazines/al-hilal)".

Ignoring every other aspect when one looks at the editorial team of "Al-Hilal" one is surprised by the personalities involved. In addition to Maulana Azad it comprised Maulana [Syed Sulaiman Nadvi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulaiman_Nadvi), Maulana Abduallah Emadi, Maulana [Abdul Salam Nadvi](https://www.rekhta.org/authors/abdussalam-nadvi/profile). Many other colleagues assisted them as needed. Every one was paid for his services. Even till date, we haven't even seen a monthly Urdu magazine with such a distinguished editorial board. Managing it for a weekly is astounding indeed. Moreover the essay writers included top notch writers of the country. Some of Maulana Shibli's best known poems appeared in "Al-Hilal" for the very first time. The point being that "Al-Hilal" turned out to be a real milestone in our political, journalistic and literary history!

Its first issue came out on 13th July 1912. Hardly three years later, the ruling British Government of India  demanded a security fee of 2000 Rupees, which was paid. When the Government could not stop the publication it increased the security to 10000 Rupees. This was way beyond Azad's financial capacity, so he shut down the paper and started a new one named "Al-Balagh". There was no real difference in the appearance and content of both these publications. In early March 1916 the Bengal Government under Defense of Indian Ordinance ordered his expulsion from the State. Since various other provinces had already issued similar decrees against him, only the provinces of Bihar and Bombay were left as his possible residence. And as Bihar was near to Calcutta offering ease of travel for people visiting him at relatively lower costs he selected Ranchi for his stay. Hardly five months living in Ranchi, on 8th July 1916 the Indian Government ordered his house arrest in Ranchi. He was released four years later on 27th December 1919.

From various aspects "Al-Hilal" proved to be an era-defining publication. No other Urdu paper before it had been published with such glory. Reality is that even papers coming out afterwards have attempted to follow its example. Every following publication has attempted to mimic "Al-Hilal" in appearance, arrangement of essays, editorial and photo journalism.

Being aesthetically pleasing with nice publication features was not what truly set "Al-Hilal" apart. The credit for that goes to the writing style of its famous editor. Never before a magazine editor  had the audacity to simultaneously call out fellow citizens, government officials, elders of the nation and religious scholars. Azad spared no one, and hardly could anyone escape his eye for accountability. Whenever he saw wrongdoing, he was quick to point it out without any fear of repercussions. Fortunately his criticism did not go unheeded; in many cases it did bring out the desired results.

He was especially harsh on the foreign Government. It is worth repeating here that "Al-Hilal" began publishing in July 1912 and including its renaming as "Al-Balagh" was shut down in 1916. Holding the mighty colonial power accountable at that time was unprecedented.  This was when, even in the annual sessions of Indian National Congress, the affirmation of loyalty to the British crown was the first resolution to be passed. Mahatma Gandhi had still not returned from South Africa; the tone of the whole political movement was very soft and friendly. Public criticism on deeds and statements of the Government actually started with "Al-Hilal". 

Journalism is generally not considered as a part of literature. However many of "Al-Hilal" essays deserve to be placed alongside the highest literary pieces. A good example is the articles Azad wrote about Muslim University which also contained a surprisingly refreshing dose of humor and sarcasm. These articles are also precious from an Urdu literature point of view. In addition to literary services of "Al-Hilal" the magazine left an indelible mark on the writers of not only its time but the next generation of poets and authors. Some of our finest writers pursued stylistic expressions which could be traced back to "Al-Hilal" and Maulana Azad. Niaz Fatehpuri comes to mind as a prominent example.

Maulana Azad's education was built upon the richness of exclusively Persian and Arabic languages. Afterwards his reading was also limited to mostly these two languages. From childhood he was very interested in oratory and speech-making. As narrated by his elder sister Fatima Begum:

"In childhood, Azad was not interested in games frequently associated with kids. At the age of seven/eight years he used to invent strange types of play; for example sometimes he used to line up all trunks and crates available in the house declaring it a train. Then he used to wear his father's turban, climbed up the trunks, sat on top and instructed his sisters to call out, 'Give way! Delhi's Maulana Saheb is arriving!' When we objected that there was nobody there. His retort was, 'this is just a game, just pretend a lot of people have come to receive me at the train station'.

Then he used to slowly come down from the trunks with measured steps, walking like a much older person. Sometimes he used to stand on a high place in the house, gather all his sisters and command them to clap for him, imagining thousands of people standing there. When I pointed out to him that except for a few of us there is nobody here, how could we imagine thousands of people with us? His response used to be; 'we are playing and in play things like this happen!'"

Not only did his love of oratory grow with time, it was also reflected in his writings. One is forced  to acknowledge that in spite of Azad's diction borrowing heavily from Arabic and Persian, neither did it lack flow, nor did it compromise attractiveness or its distinct flowery nature. On the other hand, sentences in his writing are beautifully constructed like a master craftsman polishing diamonds. 

His critics often criticize the thickness of his language due to its overreliance on difficult terms from Arabic and Persian. This is fair criticism but misses the point that in "Al-Hilal" his target audience was the learned scholarly class. Most of the topics covered in the magazine were their areas of interest. Now if one considers his own educational background combined with the knowledge and calibre of his readership, it can easily be concluded that his diction and linguistic style was indeed appropriate. He could write in a simpler language. For instance in "[Tarjuman-ul-Quran](https://archive.org/details/dli.ernet.446465)", leaving aside some parts of [Surat Fateha](https://quran.com/al-fatihah) he adopted an easy to understand language as he knew that readers interested in translation and references would be common people with possibly limited comprehension capacity of Arabic and Persian. That is why he intentionally dropped to an accessible level suited for an average reader.

Even though his own education, according to family traditions, was mainly in religious studies. He was not contended with what he was taught, instead with his vast and diverse reading landscape he significantly enhanced his knowledge in traditional subjects. In addition, he did not just rely on tradition but with contemplation and reasoning forged a new path for himself. He had a razor sharp memory which allowed him to retain much of what he read. Initially, the Quran was his main area of study, research and thinking. In "Al-Hilal" he presents Quranic topics in such an innovative and pleasing manner that made the Quran a book of daily reference as opposed to a Holy scripture reserved for special occasions.

Results of his deep understanding, introspection and learning appeared in his famous exegesis "Tarjuman-ul-Quran". It is quite sad that he was only able to publish about half of it when political activities completely consumed his intellectual bandwidth. He was not able to write the remaining parts. Without doubt it has been a great loss for the religious and scholarly world. However, since most of the fundamental islamic concepts are covered in the first half of the Quran anyway, a positive aspect is that he was able to cover interpretation of those parts in two large volumes. More importantly, if a reader pays full attention to his style of thinking and way of presentation, he would come to know how Azad wanted the Quran to be studied. This opens the possibility for readers to follow his path towards translation and interpretation of the later half. In  this sense "Tarjuman-ul-Quran" is not only an interpretation but a framework for writing a new Quranic analysis. In my view the biggest contribution of Azad's exegesis is that it completely avoids Greek philosophy and Jewish mythology. It focuses on presenting the wisdom and message of the Quran in its original scholarly and political form reflecting Azad's core belief in the purpose of the Holy Quran.

Because "Tarjumnl-ul-Quran" essentially contains translation, references and interpretation of Quranic verses, its literary value has often been overlooked. Even though this contribution is by no means insignificant.

There are numerous Urdu translations of the Holy Quran. I myself have so far seen more than ten different versions, still the fact is the  joyful narration, language precision and spontaneousness encountered in "Tarjuman-ul-Quran" is hard to find in other translations. Of course this would not have been possible if the translator did not possess equal command of both Arabic and Urdu. In addition the translator must have had a literary taste to mould the translation with prevalent Urdu mood without compromising the literal meaning. While Arabic was Azad's mother tongue, with reading and practice he developed almost native speaker-like capabilities also in Urdu. And since he had a God gifted taste for poetry and literature, all of these factors contributed in making "Tarjuman-ul-Quran" a creative marvel. 

I have already pointed above towards Azad's literary contributions in "Al-Hilal". Unfortunately, later circumstances pulled him away from the creative world. Poetry was shelved many years ago and slowly literature was also given up. With a twist of fate, during his imprisonment in Ahmednagar fort, he was able to write some literary articles in the form of letters to friends, which later appeared under the title "[Ghubar-e-Khatir](https://archive.org/details/gubar-e-khatir-abul-kalam-azad)". And while "Al-Hilal" is his youthful work with "Ghubar-e-Khatir" written in old-age, nobody can complain that it is a product of a tired old mind. On the contrary, reading it fills one with a strange sorrow realizing what a heavy price the literary world had to pay at the political altar. Had Azad focused all his energies on scholarly endeavours, who knows how many treasures could have enriched Urdu literature.
